NECRON BESTIARY

Warhammer 40,000 × Dungeons & Dragons 5th Edition — Homebrew

This bestiary catalogues the undying legions of the Necron Dynasties — ancient star-gods' servants of living metal, who slept for sixty million years and have awoken to reclaim what was theirs. Once mortal beings of flesh and ambition, the Necrontyr surrendered their bodies to biotransference and became immortal constructs of necrodermis. Now they march to reclaim their tomb worlds and exterminate those who have had the audacity to infest their galaxy while they slept. Entries are arranged from lowest to highest Challenge Rating, from the humble Canoptek Scarab to the terrifying C'tan Shards — fragments of shattered star-gods that remain among the most powerful entities in the known universe.

These are fan-made rules for personal use only.

Designer's Notes: Key Necron Mechanics

Reanimation Protocols. When a creature with this trait is reduced to 0 hit points but not disintegrated, it falls prone but does not die. At the start of each of its turns, roll a d6. On a result equal to or higher than its listed Threshold, it regains the listed HP and stands. After three consecutive failures, it is destroyed. Disintegration effects (e.g., a disintegrate spell, or a Gauss weapon's Critical Annihilation effect) bypass this ability entirely.

Gauss Weapons. Gauss weaponry strips matter layer by layer rather than relying on kinetic or thermal energy. Gauss attacks ignore resistance to their damage type if that resistance comes from nonmagical armor or natural armor. On a natural 20, the listed Critical Annihilation effect triggers — this can reduce maximum hit points and ultimately disintegrate a target, preventing resurrection.

Living Metal. Necrons constructed from advanced necrodermis regenerate automatically. A creature with Living Metal regains the listed HP at the start of each of its turns, provided it has at least 1 hit point and has not been damaged by a force, radiant, or psychic attack since the start of its previous turn.

Vehicles. Necron vehicles are fully autonomous — no crew required. They are immune to being charmed, frightened, grappled, paralyzed, petrified, poisoned, or restrained unless an effect specifically targets Huge or larger constructs. They cannot benefit from healing magic; only Living Metal, repair effects, and the mending spell (which restores 5 HP when cast on a vehicle) can restore their hit points.

Canoptek Scarab Swarm

Canoptek Scarabs are the smallest and most numerous of the Canoptek constructs — beetle-like machines barely the size of a closed fist, manufactured by Canoptek Spyders and released in clouds to serve as the Necron dynasty's tireless maintenance crew. In peacetime, they crawl across tomb world infrastructure, repairing damage, recycling broken components, and consuming organic matter that has drifted into the halls of the sleeping dead. They are mindless by design, operating on simple but ruthlessly efficient programming: consume, repair, obey.

In battle, Scarab swarms are directed at enemy forces as living dissolvers. Their mandibles can chew through plasteel plate, ceramite armor, and force fields with equal indifference. A cloud of ten thousand is a grinding, chittering death that strips a Space Marine to bare metal in minutes. Commanders who underestimate them because of their size rarely survive to learn from the mistake.

Necron Warrior

Necron Warriors are the rank-and-file of every Dynasty's legions — millions of once-mortal Necrontyr who underwent biotransference and were reborn into immortal bodies of living metal. They retain nothing of who they were. No memory, no name, no will. They are weapons that walk, processing simple combat instructions through cortex-stripped processors and carrying out the will of their Overlords with mechanical precision.

What makes the Necron Warrior truly terrifying is not its combat ability but its persistence. Gauss flayers strip flesh from bone and metal from machinery on a molecular level, and when a Warrior falls, it rarely stays down. The living metal of its body crawls back together, reknitting circuitry and reseating limbs, until the green light of its eyes blazes back to life. Enemies who leave the dead behind to advance on their objective often find those "dead" Warriors standing behind them before they reach it.

Necron Immortal

Where Warriors are the disposable infantry of the Necron legions, Immortals were the elite soldiers of the Necrontyr empire — professional warriors who chose biotransference with some measure of will intact. They retain slightly more of their ancient selves than Warriors do: enough to exercise tactical judgment, adapt to unexpected battlefield developments, and feel something that might, in a being of living metal, be called pride. They fight with the unhurried confidence of soldiers who have won ten thousand battles across sixty million years of combined memory.

Immortals wield either Gauss Blasters — heavier and longer-ranged versions of the standard Gauss Flayer — or Tesla Carbines that arc lightning between multiple targets with each hit. In either configuration, they are reliable, durable, and nearly impossible to put down permanently. A squad holding a fortified position can repel assault after assault, falling and rising and falling and rising until the attackers are gone.

Canoptek Wraith

Canoptek Wraiths are among the most unsettling things the Necron tombs produce — serpentine constructs that drift through the air on gravitic repulsors, trailing whip-like coils, their forms half-visible in the material world. They are phase-capable, meaning they can shift partially or fully into subspace, passing through solid matter as though it were mist. They were designed as tomb guardians and inner-sanctum patrol units, and in open battle that same ability makes them terrifying shock troops that no fortification can stop.

Unlike most Necron units, Wraiths operate with considerable autonomy. They are persistent in a way that feels almost personal, following their prey across entire complexes, phasing through every wall the quarry hides behind. There is no door that stops a Wraith. There is no room that is safe.

Flayed One

Among all the horrors the Necron dynasties produce, none are regarded with more disgust by the Necrons themselves than the Flayed Ones — warriors whose minds have been corrupted by the Flayer Virus, an inexplicable infection that compels them to hunt living creatures and wear their skins. They are not functioning soldiers. They are broken things, shambling through subspace, drawn by the scent of blood like insects to light, emerging in gibbering packs to fall upon whatever prey they find.

No Overlord deploys Flayed Ones. They deploy themselves, phasing out of subspace wherever the scent of carnage is strongest, driven by a hunger their metal bodies can never satisfy. The Flayer Virus has no cure, and those who have succumbed to it are regarded as a permanent shameful reminder of how thoroughly the C'tan destroyed the Necrontyr — body, mind, and soul.

Canoptek Reanimator

The Canoptek Reanimator is a four-legged repair construct built around a single function: getting fallen Necrons back on their feet as fast as physically possible. It strides through the carnage of a battle on long articulated legs, periodically extending its reanimation beam arrays downward to flood downed Warriors with the energies necessary to accelerate their living metal reconstruction. It is not a combatant by design, but any soldier who has watched one stride across a killzone under withering fire understands that "non-combatant" is a loose term when applied to a machine this durable.

Its secondary weapons — atomiser beams mounted on its arm units — are included purely as a defensive measure and fire with the mechanical disinterest of a creature that regards combat as an unfortunate interruption of its real work. Experienced commanders know that eliminating the Reanimator first is the only way to make Necron lines actually stay down.

Lokhust Destroyer

Lokhust Destroyers are Necrons whose bodies have been wholly rebuilt around the demands of their Destroyer Curse — the torso and lower frame replaced with a sleek gravitic platform mounting a heavy Gauss Cannon, the arms resculpted into weapon-brace assemblies, the mind reduced to a targeting engine with one output. They drift through the battlefield on humming suspensors, pivoting with eerie smoothness to track targets, and they are difficult in a way that goes beyond their firepower: they will not stop, they will not take cover, and they will not pursue any objective other than the destruction of the thing they are currently looking at.

The Gauss Cannon mounted on a Destroyer platform is significantly more powerful than a standard Gauss Flayer. Where the Flayer strips matter at the surface, the Cannon fires a concentrated beam capable of punching through heavy vehicle plating and reducing biological targets to molecular vapor. The Destroyer doesn't particularly care which. It just fires, pivots, and fires again.

Deathmark

Deathmarks are the Necron Dynasties' assassins — patient, methodical hunters who exist between dimensions, stalking a designated target across entire campaigns before emerging to deliver one precise, fatal shot. They are contracted out by Overlords whose enemies need to die in a specific, unavoidable way. Time means nothing to an immortal machine. A Deathmark assigned a target in the 38th Millennium will still be hunting them in the 42nd if that is what it takes.

The Deathmarks' Synaptic Disintegrators are tuned to the bioelectrical signature of their prey. Against their designated mark, Deathmarks are virtually unstoppable. They do not miss. They do not get tired. They simply wait, unseen, until the moment is perfect — and then they fire.

Hexmark Destroyer

Where most Destroyers charge into melee or close to point-blank range with their heavy weapons, the Hexmark is a stillness predator. It hovers in place, six Enmitic Disintegrator Pistols arranged in a radial array around its torso, and it waits. Anything that moves near it dies. It is a fire control problem with a body — a zone denial weapon that the Necrons built legs onto, and it does not experience impatience or boredom, because it does not experience anything except the targeting solution currently being calculated.

Enmitic Disintegrators are insidious weapons. They do not blow apart their targets. They disrupt the molecular cohesion of whatever they hit, introducing a cascade failure that spreads through the target's structure until either the target is dead or the cascade is stopped. A Hexmark that holds a corridor can kill a dozen enemies before any of them takes three steps.

Lychguard

Lychguard are the elite bodyguards of the Necron nobility — soldiers of the highest quality, selected from the mightiest Necrontyr warriors and granted the best necrodermis available. They retain substantial portions of their original personalities: their loyalty, their martial skill, their sense of personal honor. They remember what it meant to serve, and they have transferred that devotion to their Overlord without any loss of intensity.

In battle, Lychguard are almost impossible to destroy through conventional means. They place themselves between their Overlord and every threat with no hesitation, because self-preservation was never the point. The Overlord must endure. Everything else is negotiable.

In combat, Spyders are drawn to fallen Necron constructs the way a surgeon is drawn to the wounded. This makes them invaluable force multipliers, but also predictable — experienced commanders have learned to use fallen Necrons as bait, knowing the Spyder will inevitably come.

C'tan Shard of the Deceiver

If the Nightbringer is the universe's fear of death made manifest, the Deceiver is its fear of betrayal. In the War in Heaven, the Deceiver was the architect of the Necrontyr's doom — the C'tan who whispered promises of immortality in their ears and guided them toward biotransference, knowing full well what they would lose. He told them they would become gods. He was not technically lying. He simply omitted the cost. He was also the C'tan most responsible for engineering the war between the C'tan and the Old Ones, playing both sides with a patience that makes mortal manipulation look like children's games.

A shard of the Deceiver retains its creator's essential character in concentrated, maddened form. It rarely kills directly when it can convince someone else to do it. It takes genuine delight in the moment an enemy realizes they have been manipulated — the flicker of comprehension before the consequences land. If encountered in combat, it is usually because something has gone wrong with a far more elaborate plan, and it is annoyed.

C'tan Shard of the Void Dragon

The Void Dragon is the most powerful of the surviving C'tan shards and, by no small irony, currently imprisoned beneath the surface of Mars — the sacred forge-world of the Adeptus Mechanicus, the very techno-priests who worship it as the Machine God, their Omnissiah. If they knew the truth of what lay beneath their holiest soil, dreaming, they would not survive the knowledge intact. Some among the highest ranks of the Mechanicus do know. They have decided it is better to keep it contained through worship than to awaken it through alarm.

The Void Dragon's domain is technology — all technology, everywhere. It understands machines at a level that no organic mind can comprehend and no mechanical mind can resist. In its full ancient form, it could reach out and unmake every weapon, vehicle, and power system in a star system simultaneously. Even a shard, bound in necrodermis, retains the ability to corrupt and control any technological system it touches. When it wakes, and it will eventually wake, the Mechanicus's greatest weapons will become its servants — and its first act will likely be to dismantle the prison that has held it for sixty million years.
